Insulin and insulin resistance, in plain English

What insulin does, what resistance means, and why you can have insulin resistance without noticing it.

UpNext editorial · About 3 min read · October 10, 2026

Insulin helps the body use glucose

Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas, an organ inside your abdomen. Hormones are chemical messages that help different parts of the body work together. One of insulin's jobs is to help many cells take in glucose from the blood and use it for energy.

A simple way to picture it is as a signal telling cells that fuel is available. The real process involves several steps, but you do not need to know all of them to understand why insulin matters.

Resistance means a weaker response

With insulin resistance, cells in muscles, fat and the liver do not respond to insulin as well as they should. The pancreas may compensate by making more insulin. For a time, that extra effort can keep blood glucose within the usual range.

If the pancreas can no longer keep up, blood glucose may rise. It can reach the prediabetes range and, in some people, the diabetes range. This is why insulin resistance and diabetes are related but are not identical. Having one does not automatically mean having the other.

There may be no obvious symptoms

People often cannot feel insulin resistance or prediabetes. You cannot reliably identify it from how tired you feel after lunch or how much you crave a snack. Those experiences have many possible explanations. A health professional can review your history and decide which tests make sense.

Routine testing often focuses on blood glucose and A1C rather than measuring insulin resistance directly. You do not need to order a long list of tests on your own to begin a useful conversation about risk.

Several factors influence risk

Family history, age, physical activity and body fat distribution can play a role. Conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome can also be linked with insulin resistance. The combination differs from person to person. It is too simple to reduce the whole process to willpower or a single food.

Movement can help the body use glucose, and a sustainable eating pattern can support health. Weight loss may help some people with overweight or obesity, but the right goal depends on the person. Someone whose weight is already appropriate may need a different approach.

Choose a first step you can practice

You might begin with a short walk, a more regular meal routine, or replacing a sugary drink with water. These are possible starting points, not a personalized treatment plan. If you take glucose-lowering medicine, ask about any adjustments needed when changing activity or meals.
