What is A1C? The blood test with a longer view

A simple guide to A1C, common result ranges, and the questions to ask about your test.

UpNext editorial · About 3 min read · October 10, 2026

A view of the past few months

A1C is a blood test that helps show your average blood glucose over roughly the past three months. You may also hear it called HbA1c or hemoglobin A1C. It gives a longer view than a glucose reading taken at one moment, although recent weeks influence the result more than earlier weeks.

Glucose attaches to hemoglobin, a protein inside red blood cells. The A1C test measures the share of that protein with glucose attached. You do not need to remember the chemistry. The useful idea is that A1C helps reveal a pattern rather than one morning's reading.

The common ranges

For diagnosing diabetes in people who are not pregnant, an A1C below 5.7% is generally below the prediabetes range. A result from 5.7% to 6.4% falls in the prediabetes range. A result of 6.5% or higher falls in the diabetes range.

These are diagnostic ranges, not personal grades. If you have no clear symptoms, a result in the diabetes range generally needs confirmation with repeat testing. Pregnancy and some other health situations require different testing decisions, so your clinician should interpret the result with you.

A1C has limits

An average cannot show every high and low during the day. Two people can have a similar A1C and quite different glucose patterns. Someone taking insulin may therefore use home readings or a sensor alongside A1C to make day-to-day decisions.

Some types of anemia, hemoglobin variants and other conditions can make A1C less reliable. Recent blood loss or a transfusion can also affect it. Tell your healthcare professional about these circumstances, especially if the result does not seem to match your other glucose readings.

You usually do not need to fast

An A1C test itself usually does not require fasting. However, if your appointment includes other blood tests, the instructions may be different. Check with the clinic or laboratory rather than assuming that every test in the same appointment has the same preparation.

After the result, ask whether you need another test and when to repeat A1C. The interval depends on whether the test is for screening, confirming a diagnosis or monitoring diabetes treatment. There is no single schedule that fits everyone.

Ask what the result changes

Useful questions include: Does this suggest prediabetes or diabetes? Could anything make my A1C inaccurate? What should I do next? If I already have diabetes, what target is appropriate for me? A diagnosis threshold and a treatment target serve different purposes.

If your result points to prediabetes, you can explore UpNext’s Diabetes Prevention Program while arranging medical follow-up. The number is a piece of information you can use to choose a next step. It does not tell the whole story of your health.
