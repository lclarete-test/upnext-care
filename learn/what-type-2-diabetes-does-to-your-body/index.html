What Happens in Your Body When You Have Type 2 Diabetes?

Follow the connection between insulin, your pancreas and blood glucose, and understand why diabetes care involves more than avoiding sugar.

UpNext editorial · About 2 min read · October 20, 2026

EnglishPortuguêsEspañol

Start with a normal job: using energy

Glucose is a fuel your body uses. Insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas, helps many cells take glucose from the blood and use it. Type 2 diabetes develops when the body does not use insulin well and cannot make enough to meet its needs.2

The problem is not that your body should have no glucose. It is that too much remains in the bloodstream. Thinking about that process is more useful than treating every mention of sugar as something to fear.

The pancreas tries to keep up

With insulin resistance, cells respond less effectively to insulin. The pancreas can initially make more insulin to compensate. If it cannot keep up over time, blood glucose rises. Insulin resistance, prediabetes and type 2 diabetes are related, but they are not interchangeable terms.13

This is not a test of willpower. Genes and other factors influence risk, alongside activity and body fat distribution. The explanation for one person's diabetes is not a judgment about their character.3

Why you might not notice the change

Type 2 diabetes may develop slowly without obvious symptoms. When signs occur, they can include thirst, frequent urination, tiredness or blurred vision. Blood tests help identify what sensations alone cannot reliably show.2

A useful question is: “What do my tests tell us about my glucose, and what should we do next?” You do not have to understand every biological detail before asking for a clear explanation.

Why care goes beyond one number

Over time, high blood glucose can damage the body, including the eyes, kidneys and nerves. Care also addresses blood pressure, cholesterol and other risks. These are reasons for an individual care plan, not a prediction that everyone will develop complications.2

Food choices, activity, monitoring and medicines can all have a role. The combination differs between people and may change over time. Needing medicine or insulin does not mean everyday efforts were pointless.1

Make the explanation useful in daily life

Ask your care team to connect each recommendation to its purpose: What is this medicine for? What should I monitor? When is our next review? Choose a practical question you can act on today, rather than trying to redesign your whole life at once.
