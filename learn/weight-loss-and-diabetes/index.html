Weight loss and diabetes: what is the connection?

Why weight can affect diabetes risk, when weight loss can help, and why the scale is only one measure.

UpNext editorial · About 3 min read · October 10, 2026

Weight is part of the picture

Body fat, especially around the abdomen, can influence how well the body responds to insulin. For some people with overweight or obesity, losing a modest amount of weight can help improve blood glucose and lower the risk of type 2 diabetes. That does not mean weight explains every case.

People at different weights develop diabetes. Family history, age, medicines and other health factors also matter. A number on a scale cannot tell you whether someone has diabetes or how much effort they are putting into their health.

What a modest change means

For adults with overweight who are at high risk of type 2 diabetes, prevention guidance often uses a goal of losing about 5% to 7% of starting weight. For someone weighing 200 pounds, that is about 10 to 14 pounds. It is a modest change relative to starting weight, not a demand to reach a particular appearance.

The major prevention research combined weight loss with physical activity, changes to eating and structured support. Weight was one part of a broader program. The benefit should not be treated as a promise that a particular number of pounds will guarantee a particular result.

Healthy change takes more than restriction

Planned weight loss usually involves changing the balance between energy taken in and energy used over time. But the way you do that matters. A plan should provide enough nutrition and be realistic to continue. Skipping meals or cutting out many foods can be difficult to maintain and may create risks with some diabetes medicines.

A clinician or registered dietitian can help decide whether weight loss is appropriate and how to approach it. Medicines or other treatments may be options for some people. Needing additional support does not mean that everyday habits are useless.

Look beyond the scale

Walking more comfortably, preparing meals more regularly and improving a blood test can all be meaningful progress. Changes in body weight can also reflect water and other factors, so a short-term rise or fall does not tell you exactly how body fat has changed.

Unplanned weight loss is a different situation. If you lose weight without trying, especially with thirst or frequent urination, contact a healthcare professional. It can be a symptom of diabetes or another condition that needs attention.

Choose support that matches your goal

UpNext’s Diabetes Prevention Program focuses on practical changes for people who may be at risk of type 2 diabetes. Start with its eligibility information and discuss your health goals with your clinician.
