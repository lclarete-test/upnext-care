How movement helps your blood glucose

Why activity matters and how to build a routine without starting with an intense workout.

UpNext editorial · About 3 min read · October 10, 2026

Your muscles use fuel when you move

Walking, dancing, swimming and other activities make your muscles use energy. Physical activity can help the body use glucose and respond better to insulin. It can also support heart health, strength and everyday functioning. You do not need to begin with a gym membership to get moving.

Movement is useful even when the scale does not change. Weight is one possible measure of progress, but it is not the only reason to be active. Feeling stronger or finding a routine you enjoy can make it easier to continue.

Build toward a weekly pattern

A common goal for adults is at least 150 minutes of moderate activity each week. Moderate activity makes your heart beat faster and your breathing a little harder. During a brisk walk, you can usually talk but would find singing difficult.

That weekly goal can be broken into smaller sessions. If you are starting from very little activity, a short session is a reasonable beginning. Ten minutes after lunch on a few days can be more manageable than promising yourself an hour every morning.

Choose something that fits your body

A walk is one option, not the only option. Seated exercise, wheelchair rolling, water-based movement and adapted activities can also help people be active. Your abilities, health conditions and preferences should shape what you choose.

Strength activities are also valuable. Many adults aim to work major muscle groups at least twice a week, using weights, resistance bands or appropriate body-weight exercises. Start at a level that feels manageable and ask for guidance if pain, balance or other limitations make it difficult.

Diabetes medicines can change the plan

If you take insulin or medicines that can cause low blood glucose, activity can lower glucose during or after a session. Ask your care team whether you should monitor it, carry a fast-acting carbohydrate or follow particular instructions. Do not make medicine changes based on general exercise advice.

Health problems affecting your feet, eyes or heart may also change which activities are appropriate. If you have very high glucose or have been told to check for ketones, follow your clinician's plan. Exercise is not a replacement for treating a glucose emergency.

Give the habit a clear place

A useful plan names the activity, time and frequency: I will walk for ten minutes after lunch on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Then decide what you will do if it rains or your day runs late. A smaller alternative can help you keep the routine going.

Healthy Routines includes lessons on movement and building habits. UpNext’s Diabetes Prevention Program connects activity with other practical changes for people at risk of type 2 diabetes. Pick a starting point that works for your body and your week.
