What is diabetes? Start with the basics

Understand what diabetes means, how the main types differ, and what a diagnosis can tell you.

UpNext editorial · About 3 min read · October 10, 2026

A problem with how the body uses energy

Diabetes is a condition in which the amount of glucose in your blood becomes too high. Glucose is a kind of sugar that your body uses for energy. You need it to think, move, work and get through your day. The problem is not having glucose. The problem is having more of it in the blood than your body can manage.

A hormone called insulin helps many of your cells take in glucose. When the body makes too little insulin, or has trouble using it, glucose can build up in the bloodstream. That is the basic idea behind diabetes, although different types develop for different reasons.

The main types are different

In type 1 diabetes, the immune system attacks the cells that make insulin. People with type 1 need insulin treatment. It can begin in childhood or adulthood, and it is not caused by eating too much sugar.

In type 2 diabetes, the body does not respond to insulin as well as it should, and over time it may not make enough. Family history, age, activity and other factors can influence risk. People of different body sizes can develop it. Gestational diabetes develops during pregnancy and needs its own care plan.

You cannot always feel it

Some people notice thirst, frequent trips to the bathroom, tiredness or blurry vision. Others feel completely normal, especially when type 2 diabetes develops gradually. Symptoms alone cannot tell you whether you have diabetes. Blood tests and a conversation with a healthcare professional are how you get a clearer answer.

Prediabetes is a related condition: blood glucose is above the usual range but below the range used to diagnose diabetes. It raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, but progression is not inevitable.

A diagnosis is a starting point

Diabetes care can include food choices, movement, medicines, monitoring and regular checkups. The details depend on the type of diabetes and the person. Taking medicine is a useful part of care when it is needed. It does not mean that someone has failed at changing their habits.

If you are newly diagnosed, start with three questions: What type do I have? What should I do first? When should we review my progress? You do not have to learn every term or change every routine in one afternoon.
