Diabetes symptoms: what should you pay attention to?

Recognize common signs, understand why diabetes can be silent, and know when to seek urgent help.

UpNext editorial · About 3 min read · October 10, 2026

Some changes are worth checking

Feeling thirstier than usual, urinating more often, unexplained tiredness and blurry vision can be symptoms of diabetes. Frequent infections or wounds that heal slowly can also be reasons to contact a healthcare professional. These symptoms can have other causes, so noticing them is a reason to ask questions rather than diagnose yourself.

A useful detail is whether something has changed from your usual pattern. Are you getting up several times at night to urinate? Is your thirst unusual even when you drink normally? Write down when the change started and whether other symptoms appeared with it.

Weight loss can be a warning sign

Losing weight without trying, particularly alongside thirst, frequent urination or unusual hunger, deserves medical attention. When the body cannot use glucose properly because there is too little effective insulin, it may begin breaking down other energy stores.

This is different from gradual, planned weight loss as part of an appropriate health plan. Do not assume an unexplained drop on the scale is automatically a healthy change. Tell a clinician, including how much weight changed and over what period.

Diabetes can also be quiet

Type 2 diabetes may develop slowly, and some people notice few or no symptoms. Prediabetes is often silent too. Waiting until you feel sick can therefore miss an opportunity for earlier care. Testing may be appropriate because of your health history even if you feel well.

Type 1 diabetes symptoms often appear more quickly, although adults can have a slower onset. It can begin at any age. A person's age or body size cannot reliably tell you which type of diabetes they have.

Know when help is urgent

Vomiting, abdominal pain, trouble breathing, fruity-smelling breath, fainting or marked confusion can signal a serious emergency, including diabetic ketoacidosis. Seek emergency medical care if these occur, especially with thirst, frequent urination or known diabetes. You do not need to wait for a routine appointment or a home test before getting help.

For less urgent symptoms, contact your healthcare provider promptly and ask whether you should be tested. A blood test and medical assessment can help separate diabetes from other possible explanations.

Bring a short, clear description

You can say: I have been much thirstier for two weeks and I am waking up to urinate more often. That gives the clinician more useful information than saying you think your sugar is off. Mention medicines, recent illness and any family history of diabetes.
